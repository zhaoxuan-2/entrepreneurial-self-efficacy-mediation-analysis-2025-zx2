Abstract

This study examines the influence of creativity, entrepreneurial education, and entrepreneurial mindset on entrepreneurial intention among 245–246 students in the College of Business Entrepreneurship and Accountancy (CBEA) at La Consolacion University Philippines. Grounded in Albert Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory (1986), the research explores how the three independent variables influence entrepreneurial self-efficacy (mediator) and entrepreneurial intention (outcome). Using a descriptive correlational research design, data were collected through a standardized 28-item questionnaire adapted from Jiatong et al. (2021), with Cronbach's α ranging from 0.908 to 0.941. Statistical tools included weighted mean and mediated linear regression.

Findings confirm that creativity, entrepreneurial education, and entrepreneurial mindset are all at a High level, and significantly influence both entrepreneurial self-efficacy and entrepreneurial intention. Entrepreneurial self-efficacy fully mediates the creativity → intention link, and partially mediates the education → intention and mindset → intention links. Results highlight that building confidence in entrepreneurial capability is a key mechanism that transforms personal qualities and learning into stronger intention to pursue entrepreneurship.

Chapter 1 · The Problem and Its Background

Introduction

The business world is rapidly evolving, making creativity and strategic thinking vital to entrepreneurial success. All businesses begin with an idea, but not all ideas become viable business plans. Creativity enables entrepreneurs to conceive new products, services, and concepts (Brem & Puente-Díaz, 2020), while strategic thinking converts those ideas into profitable, structured business models that deliver customer value, manage resources, and compete effectively (Bashir et al., 2020). Recent research confirms that combining creativity and strategic thinking significantly boosts innovative output and adaptive capacity in shifting markets (Barkova, 2025), supporting aspiring entrepreneurs in both idea generation and problem solving amid evolving demand and competition (Remilekun et al., 2023).

Cavallo et al. (2023) add that strategic thinking improves resource allocation, decision speed, and technology adoption — raising long-run profitability. Creativity paired with strategic agility is increasingly viewed as a source of sustainable competitive advantage in fast-changing industries (Phuong et al., 2023). This study therefore investigates how creativity, entrepreneurial education, and entrepreneurial mindset shape students' intention to enter entrepreneurship, with entrepreneurial self-efficacy as the explanatory psychological mechanism.

Theoretical Framework

Albert Bandura · Social Cognitive Theory (1986)

Explains how individuals develop ability beliefs and translate them into goal-directed action. Central to the theory is self-efficacy — the conviction that one can successfully execute the behaviors required to produce given attainments. Personal factors (creativity, mindset), environmental factors (entrepreneurial education), and behavioral factors interact reciprocally. In this study, the theory frames how creativity, education, and mindset shape entrepreneurial self-efficacy, which in turn drives entrepreneurial intention.

Conceptual Framework

Statement of the Problem

The study answers the following questions:

  1. What is the level of entrepreneurial factor in terms of: 1.1 Creativity · 1.2 Entrepreneurial Education · 1.3 Entrepreneurial Mindset?
  2. What is the level of Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy and Entrepreneurial Intention among the respondents?
  3. Do Creativity, Entrepreneurial Education, Entrepreneurial Mindset significantly influence Entrepreneurial Intention and Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy?
  4. Does Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy significantly influence Entrepreneurial Intention?
  5. Does Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy mediate the relationship between Creativity / Entrepreneurial Education / Entrepreneurial Mindset and Entrepreneurial Intention?
  6. What strategy can be implemented based on the results of the study?

Hypotheses of the Study

  • H₀₁: Creativity does not have a significant influence on entrepreneurial intention.
  • H₀₂: Entrepreneurial education does not have a significant influence on entrepreneurial intention.
  • H₀₃: Entrepreneurial mindset does not have a significant influence on entrepreneurial intention.

Scope and Delimitation

The study focuses on how creativity and strategic thinking relate to business model development, and on the relationships among creative problem solving, idea generation, and the formulation of structured business models. Participants are CBEA students of La Consolacion University Philippines (BSA, BSBA MM, BSBA FM, BSBA HRDM) who have received entrepreneurial education, selected via purposive sampling. The population of 635 yielded a sample of 246 via Slovin's formula (5% margin of error). The study does not cover practicing entrepreneurs outside the university, nor long-term venture performance or profitability — only the developmental phase of business models and entrepreneurial intention.

Significance of the Study

  • Aspiring Entrepreneurs: Provides evidence on why creativity matters and how to actively develop it for competitive, sustainable ventures.
  • Businesses: Serves as reference on innovation culture and strategic agility; prioritizing both improves competitiveness and adaptability.
  • Future Researchers: Offers baseline data, conceptual grounding, and methodological reference for related entrepreneurship inquiries.

Review of Related Literature

Creativity▼

Creativity is widely recognized as a central cognitive and personality trait shaping entrepreneurial intention. Within social cognition, creativity raises perceived feasibility and desirability, influencing both intention formation and actual action (Altınay et al., 2021; Entrialgo & Iglesias, 2020). It is not limited to the arts, but is critical to idea generation, innovation, and problem solving that represents venture opportunity (Aisdl, 2020). Creativity also interacts with entrepreneurial personality, implying that deliberate cultivation can raise entrepreneurial engagement (Altınay et al., 2021).

In the Theory of Planned Behavior, creativity connects attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control to intention: more creative individuals better envision opportunities and anticipate risks (Shi et al., 2020). Entrepreneurship programs that embed creativity training consistently outperform those that do not (Wang et al., 2021; Rachmadana & Diarra, 2024). Entrepreneurial self-efficacy mediates the creativity → intention link: higher creativity builds stronger capability beliefs, which then raise intention (Ferreira-Neto et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). Creativity also fuels intrinsic motivation and entrepreneurial passion, sustaining long-term intentions.

Zhou & George (2021) developed protocols measuring idea quantity and originality in brainstorming. Zhao et al. (2022) found creativity predicts firm growth via business model innovation, especially among experienced founders. Cacciatore (2023) showed founders with artistic passions produced more unique business model configurations. Rae (2022) identified conversational leadership and liminal learning spaces as key organizational drivers of creativity in entrepreneurship education.

Entrepreneurial Education▼

Novelli & Spina (2024) position entrepreneurial education as pivotal in converting ideas into workable business models by equipping learners with knowledge, creativity, and strategic thinking. Li, Li & Lu (2022) show that evidence-based, scientific decision-making training improves idea testing, risk reduction, and smarter choices — education is not merely theoretical but trains strategic reasoning under pressure. Han, Zhou & Lu (2022) confirm education pushes students to design business models that thrive in competitive markets, with business model innovation mediating the creativity → growth link.

Kurek et al. (2023) identify strategic thinking as a key outcome: programs teach opportunity identification, risk evaluation, and adaptive planning. You (2022) highlights design thinking — brainstorming, prototyping, testing — as the hands-on process bridging raw ideas and sustainable strategies, a view echoed by the Journal of Business Economics and Management (2024). Pérez-Macías et al. (2023) add that flipped classrooms, brainstorming, and debates raise risk-taking propensity and venturing behavior.

Frontiers in Psychology (2022) highlights entrepreneurial passion and curiosity as drivers of business model innovation, while Pham et al. (2023) link entrepreneurship knowledge to digital/e-commerce venturing intention, especially with technology infusion. Tahan (2025) finds education shapes opportunity recognition and resilience despite fear of failure via psychological capital (resilience, self-confidence). Jiatong et al. (2021), Fang Li et al. (2022), Azra Bano et al. (2022), and Xiang Wang et al. (2022) collectively confirm: entrepreneurial education, creativity, and mindset together significantly raise intention, with self-efficacy as a central mediator.

Entrepreneurial Mindset▼

Degefu et al. (2024) review multiple tools for measuring mindset attitudes, intentions, and skills, concluding it is a cross-sectoral success factor. Ashraf et al. (2024) find mindset — more than alertness — mediates the education → intention link, making it critical for economic sustainability. Shabbir et al. (2025) add that mindset is built on skills and positive attitudes, which together drive intention.

Elbaz et al. (2025) confirm both education and mindset raise self-efficacy, which then strengthens intention — with creativity and role models as moderators. Oluwaseun et al. (2020) show a strong positive mindset → SME success link (unmoderated by government policy). Maqbool et al. (2021) in Pakistan/Germany find mindset improves opportunity recognition, skill building, and attitudes. Wardana et al. (2021) in Indonesia: education does not affect mindset directly — it works through student attitudes, making positive affect the key bridge. Rafaiti (2022) distinguishes an elaborating mindset (why / analytic) from an implementing mindset (how / action), noting students tend toward the former — translating knowledge into action remains a pedagogical challenge.

Ghazali et al. (2022) show Big Five traits shape self-efficacy and attitudes, which then drive intention (with passion as moderator). Handayati et al. (2020) link an entrepreneurial marketing mindset — creativity, adaptability, proactivity — to digital startup innovativeness and competitiveness. Jiatong et al. (2021) and Hadyati et al. (2020) both confirm entrepreneurship education improves mindset, self-efficacy, and intention, especially when paired with real-world tasks.

Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy▼

Deliana (2023) defines self-efficacy as belief in one's ability to succeed in specific tasks; it determines learning approach, complexity acceptance, persistence, and eventual performance. Swaramarinda et al. (2021) position it as foundational to starting and managing ventures of any size. Jiatong et al. (2021) add that self-efficacy sharpens creative thinking, clarifies cognition, and informs new-firm decisions.

Relente & Capistrano (2024) underscore its relevance to young Filipinos, whose readiness to seize opportunities depends on their innovation self-efficacy. Sarman et al. (2021) link motivation and ESE to venture creation; empathy grows when entrepreneurs trust their capabilities and take calculated risks. Wiyanto et al. (2023) and Hermio (2021) both identify ESE as a direct driver of intention formation and practical venture planning. Wardana et al. (2024) find opportunity-belief strongly predicts aspirations.

Niu et al. (2022) and Saoula et al. (2023) place ESE at the center of intention models, predicting behavioral decisions, perseverance, and learning interest. Maitlo et al. (2020) show ESE enables resource mobilization via effectuation principles (precommitments, affordable loss), supporting venture growth in developing economies. McGee & Terry (2024) find during COVID-19, ESE alone did not buffer performance shocks — but paired with strategic posture it became decisive, highlighting its contingent value in crises.

Entrepreneurial Intention▼

Cunha et al. (2022) show intention mediates the creativity → social innovation link: creative people with strong entrepreneurial perspective pursue socially useful ventures at much higher rates. Jiang et al. (2022) emphasize experiential learning (hands-on tasks, mentoring) and institutional support as critical environmental drivers. Bell & Cui (2022) document the field's shift from knowledge-only to experiential/attitude-focused entrepreneurship education.

Anjum et al. (2020) find creativity + university resources + mentorship powerfully predict student venturing. Do & Nguyen (2023) identify entrepreneurial capacity as the key mediator between education and intention, arguing for skill-based over theory-only curricula. Umar et al. (2024) extend this to faculty: perceived entrepreneurial thinking + supportive environment predict research commercialization and innovation engagement.

Huang (2023) shows innovation-focused STEM education raises intention via self-efficacy more than via personal work values. Husnah et al. (2024) link digital marketing education to innovation skills → confidence → intention. Taghizadeh et al. (2024) move to the firm level: consistent innovation-seeking intention improves both financial and operational performance. Paiva et al. (2024), in a 686-student cross-cultural study, find innovation drives intention everywhere but its strength varies culturally; environmental knowledge rarely does. Lee et al. (2022) add knowledge exploration/exploitation behaviors as intention antecedents alongside innovativeness, self-efficacy, and internal locus of control. Indhirapratha & Thavaraj (2024) provide a recent global review confirming creativity, education, and attitudes as the most replicated intention predictors.

Chapter 2 · Methodology of the Study

Research Design

Descriptive · Correlational

Systematically analyzes relationships among creativity, entrepreneurial education, mindset, self-efficacy, and intention. Descriptively captures perceptions, practices, and experiences; correlational quantifies the strength and direction of associations (Anderson & Hjertstrand, 2021).

Sampling Technique

Purposive · Slovin's Formula

Students matched to inclusion criteria (CBEA enrollees with entrepreneurial education exposure). Population N = 635; sample n = 246 at 5% margin of error: n = N / (1 + Ne²). Covers BSA, BSBA MM, BSBA FM, BSBA HRDM.

Data Gathering Procedure

  1. Request approval from the CBEA Office to administer the survey instrument.
  2. Upon approval, format the questionnaire in Google Forms.
  3. Distribute the survey link to respondents via Messenger.
  4. Allow sufficient response time; monitor participation to ensure completeness.
  5. Screen returned responses to verify every item was answered.
  6. Tally, tabulate, compute, analyze, and interpret the final dataset using SPSS.

Statistical Treatment

  • Frequency & Percentage: Demographic profile distribution.
  • Weighted Mean: Level of agreement on each variable/indicator. W = ∑(XᵢWᵢ) / n.
  • Linear / Mediated Regression: Direct, indirect, and total effects of IVs on DV via the mediator. Y = a + bX. Significance evaluated at α = .05 with 95% bootstrapped CIs.
  • Correlation Guidelines: ≥.90 Very High · .70–.89 High · .40–.69 Moderate · .20–.39 Low · ≤.19 Very Low.

Ethical Considerations

Strictly adheres to the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173). All responses are anonymized, aggregated, and stored securely. Participants may refuse any item or withdraw at any time without penalty. The study's purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits are fully communicated. Findings are reported truthfully and impartially, with no manipulation or distortion of data, and all conflicts of interest are declared.

Key Mediation Findings

1. Creativity → EI: FULL MEDIATION. Total effect significant (p=.006), but direct effect vanishes (p=.314) when ESE enters. Creativity raises intention ONLY by first building confidence.

2. Education → EI: PARTIAL MEDIATION. Both direct (B=.273, p<.001) and indirect (B=.049, p=.031) paths are significant. Education raises intention on its own AND also through confidence.

3. Mindset → EI: PARTIAL MEDIATION. Strongest a-path (B=.452), strongest total effect (B=.446), strongest indirect effect (B=.140). Mindset is the most powerful driver of both confidence and intention.

4. ESE → EI: Consistent B = 0.309, p < .001 across all models. Confidence reliably converts into intention.

All results align with Jiatong et al. (2021), Wang et al. (2023), and Sun et al. (2023). ESE functions as a key psychological mechanism that translates personal qualities and learning into behavioral intention.

Chapter 4 · Summary, Conclusions & Recommendations

Summary of Findings

  1. Demographics: Predominantly female (74%), 4th-year (35.8%), BSBA FM (39.8%), BSA (31.3%), BSBA MM (26.0%), BSBA HRDM (2.8%).
  2. Entrepreneurial factors are HIGH: Creativity WM = 3.72; Entrepreneurial Education WM = 3.98 (highest IV); Entrepreneurial Mindset WM = 3.86.
  3. Mediator & outcome are HIGH: Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy WM = 3.88; Entrepreneurial Intention WM = 3.92.
  4. All IVs → ESE and IVs → EI are significant (p < .05). Stronger creativity, education, and mindset predict both greater confidence and stronger intention.
  5. ESE → EI is significant (B = 0.309, p < .001). Greater confidence predicts stronger intention.
  6. Mediation confirmed for all three IVs:
    • Creativity → EI: Full mediation via ESE (indirect B = 0.108, p < .001; direct n.s.).
    • Entrepreneurial Education → EI: Partial mediation via ESE (indirect B = 0.049, p = .031; direct B = 0.273, p < .001).
    • Entrepreneurial Mindset → EI: Partial mediation via ESE (indirect B = 0.140, p < .001; direct B = 0.307, p < .001).
  7. Proposed strategy: Experiential learning, mentoring, business simulations, product development, pitching, and real-venture exposure — all designed to strengthen ESE as the central lever.

Conclusions

  1. Respondents manifest a consistently High level of creativity, entrepreneurial education perception, entrepreneurial mindset, self-efficacy, and intention — indicating strong entrepreneurial readiness across the board.
  2. Creativity, education, and mindset are significant positive predictors of both entrepreneurial self-efficacy and entrepreneurial intention; stronger inputs are associated with stronger confidence and stronger career intent.
  3. Entrepreneurial self-efficacy is itself a strong, reliable predictor of intention — confidence directly translates into willingness to pursue venturing.
  4. Self-efficacy is a key explanatory mechanism: it fully carries the effect of creativity onto intention, and partially carries the effects of education and mindset. Without building confidence, creativity alone does not move intention.
  5. Findings strongly support Social Cognitive Theory in the Philippine entrepreneurship education context: personal, educational, and cognitive factors interact to shape self-beliefs, which then drive behavioral intention.
  6. An integrated strategy targeting all four proximal levers — creativity, education, mindset, and especially self-efficacy — is justified to sustain and raise student entrepreneurial intention.

Recommendations

1. School & Program Administrators

Sustain and strengthen entrepreneurial factors by infusing creativity-driven and problem-solving activities across relevant subjects — idea-generation exercises, innovation challenges, real business cases — so students practice feasible solution-building, idea communication, opportunity evaluation, and risk/resource judgment.

2. Experiential & Performance-Based Learning

Continuously implement structured, hands-on opportunities: product development tasks, business pitching, mini-simulations, market-exposure projects. These concretely reinforce confidence in entrepreneurial skills and strengthen post-graduation venturing readiness.

3. Curricular Design — Beyond Theory

Intentionally connect creativity, education, and mindset development to both self-efficacy and intention. Ensure entrepreneurship instruction includes guided practice, constructive feedback, and structured reflection so learning becomes perceived capability and commitment.

4. Targeted Self-Efficacy Interventions

Because ESE directly strengthens intention, establish mentoring, coaching, and skills-building workshops on opportunity recognition, business planning, financial decision-making, and commercialization. Per Bandura's theory, prioritize mastery experiences (real tasks completed), vicarious learning (peer/mentor modeling), social persuasion (encouraging feedback), and physiological/emotional management.

5. Institutionalize the Strategy with Stakeholders

Formalize the intervention program through collaboration among administrators, faculty, and external partners. Build linkages with local entrepreneurs, business communities, and agencies for training, mentorship, and real-world exposure. Monitor progress via regular evaluation of skill development, confidence outcomes, and sustained intention.

6. Future Researchers

Conduct qualitative or mixed-method studies exploring how creativity, education, and mindset are formed; how self-efficacy translates into intention in lived experience; and which specific school/community factors shape confidence and readiness. Include in-depth interviews, FGDs, or case studies capturing barriers, supports, and personal journeys. Consider larger, more diverse samples across multiple universities to improve generalizability.
